# Photoreal car rendering and racing camera design for a three.js split-screen racer

Scope: car paint, glass/chrome/tyres/carbon/brakes, reflections, lights, car model sourcing/budgets/liveries, and racing cameras, for "Split Ways" (three.js r186 WebGLRenderer, custom per-viewport HDR pipeline, 1-4 viewports on a 4K TV, MacBook Air M1, locked 60 fps). Per-car costs multiply by up to 4 cars x 4 viewports.

Note on source quality: published primary sources from Turn 10, Polyphony and Playground on car rendering or cameras are scarce (most of their talks are members-only or not public). Concrete three.js numbers below come from the official three.js examples and from the r186 source installed in this repo (`node_modules/.pnpm/three@0.186.1`), which I read directly. Values labelled "recommended starting value" are my inferences, not sourced facts.

Current state of the project (from the repo, for context): the car is "Sports" from Rgsdev's CC0 Free Low Poly Vehicles Pack, converted to a 66 KB GLB with material groups merged from 220 to 14 draw calls ([DECISIONS.md](file:///home/user/game-gang/DECISIONS.md)). The paint is `MeshPhysicalMaterial({ metalness: 0.55, roughness: 0.34, clearcoat: 1, clearcoatRoughness: 0.05 })`, the glass is an opaque dark `MeshPhysicalMaterial` (color 0x06080d, roughness 0.04, clearcoat 1, envMapIntensity 1.4), the rims are `MeshStandardMaterial` (roughness 0.22, metalness 1), the tyres are roughness 0.92, and the env map comes from a `PMREMGenerator` in `render/sky.ts` ([carVisual.ts](file:///home/user/game-gang/client/src/games/splitways/scene/carVisual.ts)).

## 1. Car paint: MeshPhysicalMaterial settings, metallic flake, multi-layer paint (Forza/GT/Unreal/HDRP) and what is feasible in three.js WebGL vs TSL

### Takeaway
Use `MeshPhysicalMaterial` (clearcoat 1, very low clearcoatRoughness, base roughness 0.3-0.5, metalness by paint type) as the baseline, and add flakes, a two-tone "flip" colour and orange peel in a custom shader. Since r186, three.js can run TSL node materials (`MeshPhysicalNodeMaterial`) inside the existing `WebGLRenderer` through the new `WebGLNodesHandler` addon. So a TSL car paint node is now possible without moving to WebGPU, but it is new and may not pick up the project's ShaderChunk fog patches. Studio paint models (Unreal Substrate, 3Delight) are a clear coat over a coloured base plus a flake/glint layer. three.js's single clearcoat lobe over a metallic/rough base, with flakes faked through normals, roughness and metalness, gets most of the look.

### Cited Findings
**three.js baselines (official examples, MIT):**
- The official `webgl_materials_car` example uses body `MeshPhysicalMaterial({ color: 0xff0000, metalness: 1.0, roughness: 0.5, clearcoat: 1.0, clearcoatRoughness: 0.03 })`, details/rims `MeshStandardMaterial({ color: 0xffffff, metalness: 1.0, roughness: 0.5 })` and glass `MeshPhysicalMaterial({ color: 0xffffff, metalness: 0.25, roughness: 0, transmission: 1.0 })`. It uses ACESFilmic tone mapping at exposure 0.85, a `venice_sunset_1k.hdr` equirect environment, and a baked AO shadow plane (`ferrari_ao.png`, MultiplyBlending). It does not use flakes. — [three.js webgl_materials_car source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_car.html)
- The official clearcoat example's "car paint" sphere uses `clearcoat: 1.0, clearcoatRoughness: 0.1, metalness: 0.9, roughness: 0.5, color: 0x0000ff`, with `normalMap` = `FlakesTexture` via `CanvasTexture`, repeat (10, 6), anisotropy 16, `normalScale` (0.15, 0.15). There is no clearcoatNormalMap, so the flakes perturb only the base layer and the clear coat stays smooth. The same example's "Fibers" (carbon) material uses `roughness 0.5, clearcoat 1.0, clearcoatRoughness 0.1, map Carbon.png (sRGB, repeat 10x10), normalMap Carbon_Normal.png`. — [three.js webgl_materials_physical_clearcoat source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_physical_clearcoat.html)
- `FlakesTexture` is an addon that draws a canvas (default 512x512) filled with flat-normal colour `rgb(127,127,255)` and 4000 random flake splats, intended "as a normal map to produce a car paint like effect". — [three.js FlakesTexture docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/FlakesTexture.html); verified in local r186 `examples/jsm/textures/FlakesTexture.js`
- `clearcoatNormalMap` gives the clear coat its own normals, independent of the base `normalMap`. Without it the clear coat surface is smooth. This lets flakes go under a smooth coat and orange peel go on the coat. — [three.js PR #17079](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/17079)
- MeshPhysicalMaterial r186 parameters and defaults: `clearcoat` 0, `clearcoatRoughness` 0, `clearcoatNormalScale` (1,1), `iridescence` 0, `iridescenceIOR` 1.3, `iridescenceThicknessRange` [100, 400] (nm), `iridescenceMap` (R channel), `iridescenceThicknessMap` (G channel), `sheen` 0 ("for cloth and fabric"), `sheenColor` (0,0,0), `sheenRoughness` 1, `anisotropy` 0, `anisotropyMap` (R/G direction, B strength), `ior` 1.5, `specularIntensity` 1, `specularColor` (1,1,1), `reflectivity` 0.5, `retroreflectivity` 0. The docs warn that MeshPhysicalMaterial has a higher per-pixel cost and that each enabled feature adds cost. — [three.js MeshPhysicalMaterial docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)

**Custom flake shaders for three.js:**
- "demo-2025-car-paint" extends `MeshPhysicalMaterial` with flakes modelled as 3D Voronoi noise that modulate roughness and metalness (no UVs needed), a Fresnel term that changes flake colour with viewing angle, and "Orange Peel" made by perturbing the clearcoat normals with PSRD noise. Scratches were dropped because they "proved too expensive to implement in a performant way". The demo model is a Porsche 911 by Lionsharp Studios (CC BY 4.0). The repo page does not state the code licence or parameter defaults. — [GitHub: Faraz-Portfolio/demo-2025-car-paint](https://github.com/Faraz-Portfolio/demo-2025-car-paint)
- A three.js forum job post sets out what a real car paint needs beyond a static noise normal map: flakes that react dynamically and reflect the actual environment, a clearcoat normal map that affects only the clear layer, and UV-free (noise) flakes. — [three.js forum: custom carpaint shader](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/need-a-developer-for-creating-custom-carpaint-shader/7058) (older thread, pre-2024)
- Older (pre-2020) reference: a three.js recreation of the ATI Radeon 9700 car paint demo (two-tone base plus flake layer). — [2pha.com blog](https://2pha.com/blog/threejs-car-paint-shader-recreating-radeon-9700-demo/)

**TSL inside WebGLRenderer (new in r186):**
- r186 ships `examples/jsm/tsl/WebGLNodesHandler.js`, documented as a "Compatibility loader and builder for TSL Node materials in WebGLRenderer". The official example `webgl_tsl_clearcoat` imports `WebGLRenderer` from 'three', calls `renderer.setNodesHandler(new WebGLNodesHandler())`, and renders `MeshPhysicalNodeMaterial` with clearcoat, `clearcoatNormalMap` and a `FlakesTexture` normal map, using `renderer.compileAsync`. — [three.js webgl_tsl_clearcoat source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_tsl_clearcoat.html); verified in local three@0.186.1

**How multi-layer paint is modelled elsewhere:**
- Unreal Engine 5 Substrate car paint is built as a stack: clear coat on top, coloured base coat in the middle, flakes at the bottom driven by a glint density node. The base and flakes are joined with a horizontal blend and then put under the coat with a vertical layer. In Substrate, the Specular value (IOR) replaces "metallic" for reflectance. This comes from AI-generated tutorial notes, so treat node names as approximate. The same notes suggest a coat F0 of about 0.15, which is much higher than the physical clear-coat F0 of about 0.04 for IOR 1.5, so it is suspect. — [lilys.ai notes on UE 5.7 Substrate](https://lilys.ai/en/notes/unreal-engine-5-20260221/unreal-engine-substrate-materials)
- A 2024 Substrate car paint build lists the ingredients that must work together for depth: flake, orange peel, specularity, depth, colour fall-off caused by the clear coat, and the clear coat itself. — [Digital Production, Jan 2024](https://digitalproduction.com/2024/01/12/unlocking-substrate-insights-for-unreal-engines-material-system/)
- In March 2026 Epic released a free pack of 280 automotive Substrate materials for UE 5.7.3+ (about 47-50 templates, 150 setup assets). It includes single-, dual- and triple-coat paints, glints and coloured glints, thin-film interference, and imperfections (fingerprints, scratches), with separate Lumen and Path Tracer variants and emissive values in nits. It needs Substrate with Adaptive GBuffer. — [Digital Production, 2 Mar 2026](https://digitalproduction.com/2026/03/02/280-free-automotive-substrate-materials-for-ue-5-7-epic-drops-a-ready-made-lookdev-kit/)
- Unity HDRP Lit uses a "coat mask" to simulate a clear coat "to mimic Materials like car paint or plastics". The StackLit shader has a separate coat with its own smoothness. Users report that the StackLit coat cannot be masked per pixel. — [Unity HDRP Lit Shader docs (7.1.8, old version)](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/hdrp/manual/Lit-Shader.html); [Unity forum](https://forum.unity.com/threads/stacklit-coat-mask-intensity.658585/)
- Offline reference model (3Delight car paint): a flake layer suspended in the clear coat over a base, with flake density (fraction of surface showing flakes), scale and orientation randomness. Smaller flakes need more shading samples, which is the aliasing problem real time also has. — [3Delight Car Paint Material docs](https://documentation.3delightcloud.com/x/4oSvMg)
- Measured car paint BRDFs are often represented as a Cook-Torrance BRDF plus an angle-dependent colour table (the "flop" colour shift). — [USPTO patent 8,872,811 "Visualization method"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8872811)
- Polyphony Digital lists a talk titled roughly "Reproducing that car's material! BRDF fitting using a differentiable renderer" among its publications. Only the title was available. — [Polyphony Digital publications](https://www.polyphony.co.jp/publications/)
- Older (2016) mobile reference: NaturalMotion's CSR Racing 2 GDC talk covers moving to PBR in Unity with static image-based lighting, car paint simulation, an HDR-like adaptation, and "hybrid methods that create the illusion of dynamic specular reflections while keeping surfaces glossy". — [GDC Vault 2016, Liam Murphy](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023299/Delivering-Console-Car-Visuals-on)

### Inferences
- **Recommended starting presets** (my inference, tuned against the cited baselines; test under the game's ACES and exposure):
  - *Solid gloss (non-metallic)*: `metalness 0, roughness 0.35-0.45, clearcoat 1, clearcoatRoughness 0.02-0.04`, no flakes. A black or white solid paint is where clear-coat reflections matter most.
  - *Metallic*: `metalness 0.6-0.85, roughness 0.30-0.45, clearcoat 1, clearcoatRoughness 0.02-0.04`. Put the flake normal on the base `normalMap` (normalScale 0.05-0.15, as in the official example) and keep the coat smooth, or give it a very weak orange-peel `clearcoatNormalMap` (normalScale about 0.02-0.05).
  - *Pearlescent / colour-shift*: the metallic preset plus `iridescence 0.3-1.0, iridescenceIOR 1.3-1.8, iridescenceThicknessRange` set to about [250, 600] or wider to push hue shift. Better: a custom face/flop colour ramp (`mix(faceColor, flopColor, pow(1 - N·V, k))`) in TSL or onBeforeCompile, which is what the patent's angle-dependent colour table describes.
  - *Matte/satin*: `clearcoat 0, metalness 0-0.3, roughness 0.55-0.7`.
  - Do not use `sheen` for paint. The docs describe it as a cloth/fabric layer.
- **Flake aliasing at 1080p/4K split-screen**: real flakes (tens of microns) are far below pixel size. A tiled flake normal map at distance either shimmers or mip-averages to a flat normal. Make the flake contribution fade with distance or screen-space derivative (or bake it into roughness at distance), and keep flakes as a sparkle term rather than a strong normal. This is what "glint density" in Substrate and density/scale in 3Delight address.
- **Implementation path, in order of risk**: (1) Shared `FlakesTexture` or custom flake normal map, plus a per-paint `clearcoatNormalMap` orange peel, using stock `MeshPhysicalMaterial`. No new shader code, so it is the safest. (2) `onBeforeCompile` injection of a view-dependent flop colour and procedural (triplanar or object-space) flakes, like the Faraz demo, so it keeps working with the project's custom ShaderChunk fog. (3) TSL `MeshPhysicalNodeMaterial` via `WebGLNodesHandler`. This is the cleanest authoring and is forward-compatible with WebGPU, but it is new in r186. Check that node materials honour the project's patched fog chunks, MSAA HDR target and shadow setup before committing.
- Keep paint material count low: one paint material per player (colour and preset uniforms) and shared materials for everything else, as the code already does. Shader variants multiply compile time and draw-call state changes across 4 viewports.

### Gaps
- No primary source found on Forza Motorsport's or Gran Turismo 7's real-time car paint model (number of lobes, flake implementation). The Polyphony talk is title-only.
- No published GPU cost numbers for MeshPhysicalMaterial clearcoat, iridescence or a TSL flake shader on Apple M1. Measure in-game.
- The Unity HDRP docs found are an old version (7.1.8). Current HDRP StackLit/AxF car-paint docs were not retrieved.

## 2. Glass, chrome and rims, tyres (sidewall lettering), carbon fibre, brake discs

### Takeaway
Avoid `transmission` on car glass in split screen. In three.js r186 every camera that sees a transmissive object triggers an extra opaque render pass into a per-camera render target sized to the viewport. Use opaque or alpha-blended dark tinted glass with clearcoat instead. Chrome and rims are metalness 1 with low roughness and depend on a good env map. Brake discs and machined rims can use `anisotropy`. Carbon fibre has an official recipe. Tyres need high roughness, near-black albedo, and normal-mapped sidewall lettering from a shared atlas.

### Cited Findings
- In r186 `WebGLRenderer`, if a camera's render list has transmissive objects, `renderTransmissionPass(opaqueObjects, transmissiveObjects, scene, camera)` runs. It lazily creates `transmissionRenderTarget[camera.id]` and sizes it to `activeViewport.z * transmissionResolutionScale` by `activeViewport.w * transmissionResolutionScale` (default scale 1.0), re-rendering the opaque objects into it. — [three.js WebGLRenderer.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/src/renderers/WebGLRenderer.js) (verified in local three@0.186.1, lines ~297, 1764-1782, 2002-2044)
- The official car example uses glass with `transmission: 1.0, metalness 0.25, roughness 0`. It is a single-viewport demo. — [three.js webgl_materials_car source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_car.html)
- `transmission`: "When non-zero, set opacity to 1". `dispersion` only works with transmission. `anisotropy` (0-1) is for brushed metals, with `anisotropyMap` R/G giving the tangent-space direction and B the strength. `retroreflectivity` (0-1) exists in r186. — [three.js MeshPhysicalMaterial docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)
- Carbon fibre (official): `roughness 0.5, clearcoat 1.0, clearcoatRoughness 0.1, map Carbon.png (sRGB, repeat 10x10), normalMap Carbon_Normal.png`. — [three.js webgl_materials_physical_clearcoat](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_physical_clearcoat.html)
- The official car example uses `metalness 1.0, roughness 0.5` for rims and trim. — [three.js webgl_materials_car](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_car.html)
- The `DecalGeometry` addon projects decal meshes onto geometry, but its source warns that "decal projections can be distorted when used around corners" (issue #21187). — [three.js DecalGeometry.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/examples/jsm/geometries/DecalGeometry.js) (verified in r186)
- Epic's 2026 automotive pack includes shared material functions for anisotropy, imperfections, emissive (in nits) and perforation (grilles and mesh), which shows the categories a car material set needs. — [Digital Production, 2026](https://digitalproduction.com/2026/03/02/280-free-automotive-substrate-materials-for-ue-5-7-epic-drops-a-ready-made-lookdev-kit/)

### Inferences
- **Glass (recommended)**: keep the current opaque dark glass for chase and TV cameras. It already uses a dark colour, near-zero roughness and clearcoat, which reads as tinted glass and reflects the sky. For bumper or cockpit cams, swap the windscreen to `transparent: true, opacity 0.15-0.3, depthWrite: false` with clearcoat for reflections, never `transmission`. With 4 viewports, transmission would add up to 4 extra full opaque passes per frame.
- **Chrome**: `metalness 1, roughness 0.02-0.08, color ~0xf2f2f2`. **Painted or alloy rims**: `metalness 1, roughness 0.2-0.35`. Machined lips and brake discs: `anisotropy 0.5-0.8` with a radial or concentric `anisotropyMap`, which needs tangents or the map.
- **Brake discs**: `metalness 1, roughness 0.35-0.5` with a concentric anisotropy map. Add a heat-glow emissive ramp (dark red to orange, intensity driven by brake temperature from the physics) through the HDR bloom pipeline.
- **Tyres**: `roughness 0.85-0.95`, near-black albedo (sRGB about 0x151515-0x1c1c1c; pure black looks fake under ACES). Put sidewall lettering (our own fictional tyre brand) in a shared 1K tyre atlas as normal map plus slight roughness/albedo change on raised letters, mapped by a radial UV on the sidewall ring, plus an optional dust/brake-dust gradient toward the rim. Tread can be normal-only on the hero LOD. Use one tyre material for all cars.
- **Carbon fibre**: the official recipe plus `anisotropy` along the weave (anisotropyMap) for the angle-dependent sheen of real carbon. Keep the weave texture tiled and mipmapped to avoid moiré at distance.
- **Lights glass**: headlight and tail-light covers work best as a clearcoat-like transparent layer over an emissive or chrome reflector mesh. Without transmission, fake depth with a reflector normal map.

- No sourced numeric values from racing studios for tyre, rim or glass materials.
- No measured cost of anisotropy or iridescence on M1/ANGLE-Metal.

## 3. Reflections: HDRI + PMREM vs dynamic CubeCamera, SSR, and what racing games do

### Takeaway
Commercial racing games use dynamic cube maps (Forza 7 on Xbox One X/PC) or ray tracing (Forza Motorsport 2023), because a static sky reflection does not "seat the car in the world". For a 4-viewport browser game, layer it: (a) the existing HDRI PMREM as a fallback, (b) baked PMREM reflection probes placed along the track spline at load time (no per-frame cost), and (c) on higher presets, one or two time-sliced dynamic `CubeCamera`s (one face per frame, 128-256 px, half-float) for the cars nearest the camera. SSR is a poor fit for 4 viewports.

### Cited Findings
- Forza Motorsport 7 (2017) made dynamic cube mapping exclusive to Xbox One X and Windows PC. Creative director Bill Giese said it helps "better seat the cars in the world". Dynamic cube maps mean rendering six faces, which is six extra scene renders per update. — [GamingBolt (older, 2017)](https://gamingbolt.com/forza-motorsport-7-features-dynamic-cube-mapping-exclusively-on-xbox-one-x); [Wccftech (2017)](https://wccftech.com/turn-10-forza-motorsport-7-enhancements/)
- Forza Motorsport (2023) uses ray tracing for reflections and ambient occlusion. — [GamingBolt graphics analysis](https://gamingbolt.com/forza-motorsport-graphics-analysis-a-new-visual-benchmark-for-racing-games). A forum post relaying Digital Foundry says car reflections are a hybrid of RT and cube maps depending on distance (unconfirmed). — [Guru3D forum](https://forums.guru3d.com/threads/forza-motorsport.446458/page-4)
- On PC, Forza Motorsport (2023) exposes a "Cubemap reflections" quality setting, and lowering it to Medium "notably" improves frame rates. Cube map reflections are a significant cost even on a AAA renderer. — [OC3D performance guide](https://overclock3d.net/reviews/software/forza-motorsport-pc-performance-review-and-optimisation-guide/4/)
- The three.js `CubeCamera` renders the scene into a `WebGLCubeRenderTarget` through `update(renderer, scene)`. The docs example uses a 256 cube target with `generateMipmaps: true`, hides the car, positions the cube camera at the car, updates, then shows the car again. It also has `activeMipmapLevel`. — [three.js CubeCamera docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/CubeCamera.html)
- The official dynamic cube map example uses `new WebGLCubeRenderTarget(256)` with `texture.type = HalfFloatType` and calls `cubeCamera.update(renderer, scene)` every frame. — [three.js webgl_materials_cubemap_dynamic](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_materials_cubemap_dynamic.html)
- In r186 `PMREMGenerator`, `fromScene(scene, sigma, near, far, { size })` defaults to `size = 256`. Equirect input is ideally 1k (1024x512) "as this matches best with the 256x256 cubemap output", and cube input is ideally 256x256 per face (minimum 16x16). — [three.js PMREMGenerator.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/src/extras/PMREMGenerator.js) (verified in r186)
- Unity's reflection-probe performance docs: higher cubemap resolution means longer render time. Time-slicing spreads the six faces and their blur/convolution passes over several frames, trading update latency for per-frame cost. — [Unity 6.2 manual: Reflection probe performance](https://docs.unity3d.com/Manual/RefProbePerformance.html)
- three.js forum advice on CubeCamera: hide the reflecting object during capture, update only when something moved, and use half-float with mipmaps. Runtime env mapping "doesn't run well on every device". — [GDevelop/three.js forum thread](https://forum.gdevelop.io/t/with-three-js-3d-reflections/47060)
- r186 adds `LightProbeGridWebGL`, "a 3D grid of L2 Spherical Harmonic irradiance probes that provides position-dependent diffuse global illumination" for WebGLRenderer, with fully GPU-resident baking (cubemap render, SH projection, packing, no CPU readback). — [three.js LightProbeGridWebGL.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/examples/jsm/lighting/LightProbeGridWebGL.js) (verified in r186; example `webgl_lightprobes` "light probe volume")
- three.js has screen-space reflection examples (`webgl_postprocessing_ssr`; WebGPU `webgpu_postprocessing_ssr`, `webgpu_postprocessing_ssr_denoise`, `webgpu_reflection_roughness`). — [three.js examples list (files.json)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/files.json)

### Inferences
- **Cost model**: a full dynamic cube update is 6 extra scene renders. With 4 cars, a naive per-car, per-frame update is 24 extra scene renders per frame on top of 4 viewports, which is impossible at 60 fps on an M1 at 4K. Time-sliced (1 face per car per frame), it is 4 face renders per frame and each cube refreshes every 6 frames (100 ms at 60 Hz), plus a PMREM `fromCubemap` per refresh. That is still expensive, so reserve it for 1-2 player presets.
- **Recommended layered approach**:
  1. *Low preset*: HDRI PMREM only (already in place). Add a fake ground hemisphere (asphalt-grey lower half) to the PMREM source scene, so the lower body and sills reflect road instead of sky.
  2. *Medium (default for 3-4 players)*: bake N reflection probes along the track spline at load (every 75-150 m, plus at bridges and tunnels), each `PMREMGenerator.fromScene` at 128-256 with track geometry and environment but no cars. Each car picks the nearest probe and crossfades to the next (a two-envMap mix needs a small onBeforeCompile/TSL change, or swap and fade `envMapIntensity`). Zero per-frame render cost, and reflections show grandstands, trees and tunnel ceilings, which is where most of the "seated in the world" look comes from.
  3. *High (1-2 players)*: one shared time-sliced dynamic cube (1 face per frame, 128-256 px, HalfFloat, scene rendered on a reflection layer with lowest LODs, no particles or post, other cars included) placed at each viewport's own car. That car gets live reflections of opponents, and other cars keep the baked probes.
  4. SSR: skip for multi-viewport. Each viewport would need its own depth/normal pre-pass and SSR pass, and screen-space reflections miss off-screen content, which is most of a car's reflection.
- Diffuse lighting: `LightProbeGridWebGL` (baked once per track) can darken the car body and underside in tunnels and under trees cheaply, complementing specular probes.
- Feed reflections through the existing HDR pipeline (half-float). Clamp very bright sun texels in the probe source to avoid fireflies on clearcoat at 4K.

### Gaps
- No public Turn 10, Polyphony or Playground talk detailing cube map resolution, update rate or probe placement was found. Their exact budgets are unknown.
- No measured cost of `CubeCamera.update` or `PMREMGenerator.fromCubemap` on an M1 in Chrome. Benchmark in-game.

## 4. Lights: headlights and taillights (emissive + bloom), light cones, lens flares, brake lights

### Takeaway
Do car lights as emissive materials pushed above 1.0 into the existing HDR bloom, not as real lights. Real three.js lights add per-fragment cost to every lit material in every viewport, and changing light counts recompiles shaders. If a night track arrives, use at most one shadowless `SpotLight` with a cookie (`.map`) per local player car, and fake cones and glows with additive meshes and sprites. The stock `Lensflare` addon reads back the framebuffer, which is fragile with an MSAA half-float pipeline. Prefer flare and streak sprites in the composite pass.

### Cited Findings
- r186 `SpotLight` supports a projected texture: `spotLight.map = new THREE.TextureLoader().load(url)`, with cookie-like masking. — [three.js SpotLight.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/src/lights/SpotLight.js) (verified in r186)
- The r186 `Lensflare` addon uses two 16x16 `FramebufferTexture`s (temp and occlusion) and calls `renderer.copyFramebufferToTexture` to test occlusion. It respects `renderer.getCurrentViewport`, so it can work per viewport. The official example adds `LensflareElement`s of sizes 700, 60, 70, 120 and 70 at distances 0-1. — [three.js Lensflare.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/examples/jsm/objects/Lensflare.js); [webgl_lensflares](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_lensflares.html) (verified in r186)
- three.js has a WebGPU-only post-process lens flare (`webgpu_postprocessing_lensflare`) and an IES spotlight example (`webgpu_lights_ies_spotlight`). — [three.js examples list](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/files.json)
- MeshPhysicalMaterial r186 has `retroreflectivity` (0-1), with a WebGPU example `webgpu_materials_retroreflection`. — [three.js MeshPhysicalMaterial docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html); [examples list](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/files.json)
- Epic's 2026 automotive material pack specifies emissive values as physical luminance in nits, which shows that AAA pipelines calibrate light intensity rather than eyeballing it. — [Digital Production, 2026](https://digitalproduction.com/2026/03/02/280-free-automotive-substrate-materials-for-ue-5-7-epic-drops-a-ready-made-lookdev-kit/)
- A browser driving-game chase-camera PR notes that follower cars' headlights stay "clearly visible from 60 m back, thanks to a small glow that stays a few pixels across at any distance". A constant-pixel-size glow sprite keeps lights readable at range. — [GitHub ashciraulo/500 PR #20](https://github.com/ashciraulo/500/pull/20) (small hobby project, low authority)

### Inferences
- **Recommended emissive ladder** (linear HDR, before ACES; tune with the bloom threshold): tail running light about 1.5-2.5; brake light about 6-10, ramping in about 50 ms and out about 150 ms; headlight lens about 4-8 by day and 8-15 at night; reverse light about 4. Use a separate taillight material per car (already done) and drive `emissiveIntensity` from brake input. A brake-light flash is one of the strongest readability cues in split screen.
- **Light cones and glows**: an additive, depth-tested, fog-aware cone mesh with fresnel edge fade and distance falloff for headlight beams (night or dusk only), plus a camera-facing glow sprite with a minimum pixel size for distant cars. No real lights for opponents.
- **Lens flares**: the stock `Lensflare` uses `copyFramebufferToTexture`. In WebGL2 copying from a multisampled framebuffer is not allowed, so it likely fails or needs a resolve step in this MSAA HDR pipeline (inference, not tested). Instead, add sun and headlight flare ghosts and an anamorphic streak in the composite shader, with occlusion from a depth-buffer sample at the light's screen position.
- **Real lights budget**: for a night track, one `SpotLight` per local player's own car with a cookie map and no shadows (or a 512 shadow map on the highest preset). Bake trackside lighting into lightmaps or vertex colours.
- Number plates, reflectors and signs can use `retroreflectivity` so they flash in headlights cheaply. Check that it is supported on the WebGL path.

### Gaps
- No primary source from Forza or GT on how they render headlight cones or brake-light bloom.
- The MSAA plus `copyFramebufferToTexture` conflict is my inference and was not tested.

## 5. Car models: hero vs opponent triangle counts, LODs, texture sizes, upgrading Speed Dreams vs sourcing CC0/CC-BY fictional models, livery/decal system

### Takeaway
Do not upgrade the 2009 Speed Dreams meshes (4-6k triangles). Clearcoat reflections expose faceting, and their topology is unsuitable. Use them only as dimension and handling references (wheelbase, track, mass). Free CC0 packs (Kenney, Quaternius, RG Poly, the current Rgsdev pack) are stylised low-poly and will not reach Forza fidelity. The realistic options are (a) CC-BY Sketchfab models of original or fictional designs, with attribution, (b) paid Fab Standard License models (commercial use, no attribution, revenue-tiered pricing), or (c) commissioning or modelling 4-6 original "GTA-style" bodies. Avoid models of real cars even under CC-BY, because Creative Commons licences do not grant trademark rights.

### Cited Findings
**Free and open sources:**
- Kenney Car Kit: over 40 models (cars, trucks, vans) in OBJ, FBX and glTF, CC0 1.0. Commercial use allowed, no attribution required. Stylised, not sports cars. — [Kenney Car Kit (itch.io)](https://kenney-assets.itch.io/car-kit); Kenney Toy Car Kit (CC0, stylised toy vehicles) — [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/content/toy-car-kit)
- Quaternius Lowpoly/Realistic Car Pack (Nov 2018): CC0, FBX/OBJ/Blend only (no glTF), one model Patreon-only. — [Quaternius (itch.io)](https://quaternius.itch.io/lowpoly-cars)
- RG Poly "Cars - Low Poly Massive": CC0, free for personal and commercial use, 40 models including sports cars. The free version has 26 cars in FBX and glTF. — [RG Poly (itch.io)](https://rg-poly.itch.io/cars-low-poly-massive-car-pack)
- An OpenGameArt "sports car (lowpoly)" is CC0 but only 358 triangles and untextured. — [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/content/sports-car-lowpoly)
- Sketchfab licences: CC BY allows commercial use with credit, CC BY-NC forbids commercial use. — [Sketchfab help: licences](https://help.sketchfab.com/en/articles/16152220). **Decals:**
- `DecalGeometry` projections distort around corners (three.js issue #21187). — [three.js DecalGeometry.js](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/examples/jsm/geometries/DecalGeometry.js)

### Inferences
- **Budgets for 4 cars x 4 viewports on an M1 at 60 fps** (my inference; no racing-studio numbers were found):
  - LOD0 (own car in its own viewport, or car within about 15 m): 40-80k triangles, at most 8-10 draw calls (paint, glass, trim/black plastic, chrome/metal, lights, tyres, rims, interior). Wheels as instanced or shared geometry.
  - LOD1 (15-60 m): 12-20k triangles, 4-6 draw calls.
  - LOD2 (60-150 m): 3-6k triangles (Speed Dreams-class), 2-3 draw calls.
  - LOD3 (over 150 m): under 1k triangles, 1 draw call, or the existing merged silhouette proxy.
  - Worst case (4 viewports, every car at LOD0 in every viewport) is 16 x 80k = 1.3M triangles and about 160 draw calls for cars alone, plus shadow passes. Use the LOD per viewport (distance to that viewport's camera), not globally. Fragment cost (MeshPhysical clearcoat at 4K) will dominate more than triangles.
  - Textures: one 2048 livery/paint mask per car (or 1024 on low preset), one shared 2048 "details" atlas (badges, grilles, lights, interior), one shared 1024 tyre atlas, and a shared 512-1024 flake/orange-peel normal. Compress with KTX2/Basis (UASTC for normal maps, ETC1S for colour). On a 16 GB M1 memory is not the constraint; download size and decode time are.
- **Upgrade vs replace**: Speed Dreams bodies are too coarse for modern specular. Subdividing them gives soft, wrong shapes, and remodelling costs about as much as a new model. Use their specs (dimensions, mass, aero, torque curves) and model new bodies.
- **Model plan for fictional GTA-style brands**: (1) Prefer original-design cars, either CC-BY with an attribution screen, or Fab or marketplace royalty-free licences with no attribution. Verify per asset that the design is not a real car, and remove all real badges and number-plate text. (2) Or commission 4-6 base bodies (hatch, coupé, GT, supercar, muscle, prototype) at LOD0 about 60k triangles with LODs, with a livery UV2 baked in. This is the most legally clean route for a subscription product, and the bodies can be reshaped into variants (bumpers, wings).
- **Livery/decal system** (recommended): give each body a non-overlapping livery UV set (UV1). At runtime, composite a 2048 livery texture per car from vector layers (base colour regions, stripes, number, our own SVG brand logos and sponsors) into a canvas or render target, then read it in the paint shader as base colour (and optionally a metallic/flake mask) under the clearcoat, so decals get the same coat reflection. This avoids `DecalGeometry` distortion and extra draw calls. Keep the brand logos as SVG source files we own.

### Gaps
- No public triangle or texture budgets from Forza, GT, Codemasters or other web racers were found for hero vs opponent cars.
- I could not find a specific, verified CC0/CC-BY realistic (non-stylised) original-design car with LODs. Most realistic free models are real trademarked cars. This needs a manual Sketchfab and Fab search with licence checks.
- The Fab EULA terms on web delivery (an asset shipped as a downloadable GLB in a browser client) were not checked. Read the full EULA for any "reasonable protection against extraction" clause before buying.
- The licences of the Speed Dreams 3D models themselves were not verified individually. Only the data is assumed Free Art License per the brief.

## 6. Racing cameras: chase cam (spring arm, damping, FOV vs speed, look-ahead, height), bumper/hood, cockpit, replay/TV, camera shake; published talks

### Takeaway
Make the chase cam a car-relative rig with critically damped springs on the offset in the car's local frame, not world-space lag (which causes several metres of drift at speed). Add speed-scaled height, distance and FOV, yaw look-ahead into corners, a slight lateral slip offset, and trauma-based Perlin shake for impacts, kerbs and high speed. The best public racing-camera talk is Criterion's GDC 2018 "Vehicle Feel Masterclass", but its content is members-only. Turn 10, Playground and Codemasters camera talks were not found.

### Cited Findings
**Published talks and articles:**
- Criterion Games, GDC 2018, Matthew Harris, "Vehicle Feel Masterclass: Balancing Arcade Accessibility with Simulation Depth": a solid physical simulation plus layers on top, drawing on 16 years of handling work (Burnout, Need for Speed), explicitly including "how the camera moves" and handling assists. The full talk is members-only. A YouTube upload exists. — [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025383/Vehicle-Feel-Masterclass-Balancing-Arcade); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n_A0RqeGado) (older, 2018)
- Squirrel Eiserloh, GDC 2016, "Math for Game Programmers: Juicing Your Cameras With Math": a 0-1 "trauma" value that decays over time, shake amplitude as an exponential (squared or cubed) function of trauma, and smooth noise (Perlin) for translational and rotational offsets. It also covers split-screen and framing. — [Game Developer summary](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/video-sprucing-up-cameras-with-math); [Bevy 2D screen-shake example implementing it](https://bevy.org/examples/camera/2d-screen-shake/); [three.js forum port](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/camera-shake-or-much-damage-such-wow/8963) (older, 2016)
- Holm, Skovhus and Kraus (2017), "Increasing the Perceived Camera Velocity in 3D Racing Games through Camera Attributes and Position", Springer LNICST vol. 196, pp. 121-128, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-55834-9_14. The abstract and findings were not retrievable. — [AAU record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/increasing-the-perceived-camera-velocity-in-3d-racing-games-throu)
- A game-feel survey notes a tight link in racing games between field of view, motion blur and perceived speed. — [Designing Game Feel: A Survey (arXiv 2011.09201)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.09201)

**Springs and damping:**
- Allen Chou's numeric springing: parameterise springs by oscillation frequency and damping ratio (ζ, ω) instead of hand-tuned stiffness. Implicit Euler is always stable, while semi-implicit can blow up. He also covers half-life parameterisation. — [Allen Chou: Precise Control over Numeric Springing (2015)](https://allenchou.net/2015/04/game-math-precise-control-over-numeric-springing); [More on Numeric Springing](https://allenchou.net/2015/04/game-math-more-on-numeric-springing/)
- Unreal Engine ships `CriticalSpringDamper` / `CriticalSpringDampFloat` (critically damped smoothing by smoothing time) and a double-spring variant with a more S-shaped response. — [Unreal API: CriticalSpringDamper](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Core/SpringMath/CriticalSpringDamper)
- Frame-rate-independent damping needs an exponential factor. A fixed lerp feels sluggish at 30 fps and twitchy at 144 fps. — [Moonjump: Third-Person Camera Systems](https://moonjump.com/game-dev-mechanics-third-person-camera-systems-how-it-works/)
- Little Polygon's third-person camera breakdown recommends a critically damped spring or low-pass filter on the tracked position. On Solar Ash (character at about 45 mph) they skipped horizontal smoothing because it made the camera lag. — [Little Polygon: Third Person Cameras](https://blog.littlepolygon.com/posts/cameras/)
- Unreal's "Six ingredients for a dynamic third person camera" exposes look-ahead distance as a designer-tunable property, alongside rotation speed and raycast whiskers. — [Unreal Engine tech blog](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/tech-blog/six-ingredients-for-a-dynamic-third-person-camera)

**Concrete browser chase-cam evidence (hobby projects, low authority but measured):**
- World-space smoothing left a chase camera about 4 m further back than intended at 120 km/h, at roof height, hiding the road 20-27 m ahead. Smoothing the offset in the car's frame fixed it. The camera now rises up to 0.45 m and aims up to 7 m further ahead with speed. Hidden road ahead dropped from 26.7 to 10.7 m at 85 km/h and from 17.6 to 11.2 m at 50 km/h. The same PR adds a look-behind that cuts instantly, a free-look orbit that keeps the car centred, and a wall check that starts above the car so crests do not pull the camera low. — [GitHub ashciraulo/500 PR #20](https://github.com/ashciraulo/500/pull/20)
- Another project measured spring-arm lag as speed divided by damping (about 9 m extra at 220 km/h) and fixed it by easing the boom offset relative to the target. It added speed-based distance, pitch follow, look-back, boom collision and FOV kicks on events like landings. — [GitHub Noisemaker111/jgengine PR #1802](https://github.com/Noisemaker111/jgengine/pull/1802)
- A racer project's camera spec lists the features to tune: camera follow, lateral lag, steering look-ahead, curve anticipation, horizon response, and a configurable FOV illusion, all tunable without editing scene code. — [GitHub rwrife/neon-racer issue #6](https://github.com/rwrife/neon-racer/issues/6)

**Player and community expectations:**
- Forza players argue that a static chase cam makes corners feel like the track is moving, and that dynamic camera motion is key to the sense of speed. Another thread notes that at speed some cars are almost half cut off by Forza's chase camera placement. — [Forza forum: chase cam vastly under-rated](https://forums.forza.net/t/forzas-chase-cam-is-vastly-under-rated/541156); [Forza forum: exterior camera placement](https://forums.forza.net/t/whats-the-matter-with-third-person-exterior-camera-placement/555133)
- GT7 offers chase-cam sensitivity and axis-offset settings. Players discuss the car appearing to turn before the camera follows. — [GTPlanet forum](https://www.gtplanet.net/forum/threads/chase-cam-is-ruining-gt7-for-me.405045/)
- Project Motor Racing players note that FOV strongly changes how the chase cam feels and leave it at the default 60. — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/299970/discussions/0/687492429636460744/)
- The Gran Turismo film built a camera rig so the entire car fits in frame, imitating the game's chase cam. — [Looper](https://www.looper.com/1274215/gran-turismo-pulled-off-games-chase-camera-movie)
- Wider views add visual speed cues, so chase and hood cams feel faster than cockpit. — [MakeUseOf (older, about 2021)](https://www.makeuseof.com/how-to-optimize-racing-games/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended chase-cam rig** (starting values to tune; not sourced from a studio):
  - The target is a point on the car about 1.0-1.2 m up and 1.5-3 m ahead of the centre of mass (more look-ahead at speed). The camera sits at an offset in the car's yaw frame: distance 5.5-6.5 m at rest, rising to 6.5-7.5 m at top speed, height 1.6-2.0 m rising by about 0.3-0.5 m with speed, pitch about 6-10° down.
  - Smoothing: critically damped springs (half-life parameterised, frame-rate independent) on (a) yaw relative to the car's heading (half-life about 0.12-0.25 s, so slides show the car's angle), (b) height and pitch (to soak up kerbs and suspension), and (c) distance (a small "pull-back" on acceleration and "push-in" on braking). Never smooth absolute world position. Follow velocity direction partly (about 20-35%) instead of pure heading, so drifts read clearly.
  - Look-ahead into corners: yaw the aim point toward the steering and yaw-rate direction (a few degrees) and toward the track spline tangent 20-40 m ahead. The project has the spline, which is a big advantage over generic look-ahead.
  - FOV vs speed: base vertical FOV about 55-60° (at 16:9), rising by 8-15° toward top speed with an eased curve, plus short FOV kicks for boost or slipstream and landings. Combine with the existing speed lines and vignette. Keep FOV changes slow (about 0.3-0.5 s) to avoid motion sickness on big TVs.
  - Split screen: in 2-player side-by-side (each about 8:9) keep horizontal FOV close to the 16:9 value (Hor+) or switch to top-and-bottom layout (32:9 strips) with a lower, closer camera. In 4-player quadrants each viewport is 16:9, so use the standard values.
  - Collision and crests: start the boom raycast from above the car (as in PR #20) so crests do not pull the camera low. On the mountain road, use the spline to pre-raise the camera before blind crests.
- **Bumper/hood cam**: camera rigidly attached at the bumper or bonnet, FOV about 65-75°, a slight smoothed pitch and roll from body motion (10-30% of chassis roll) and road-texture shake from suspension velocity. Fastest sense of speed, so a good default for 1-2 player "pro" mode.
- **Cockpit cam**: needs interiors, which roughly doubles LOD0 cost. Defer, or offer only in 1-player mode.
- **Camera shake** (Eiserloh trauma): trauma from collisions, kerb strikes, off-track surfaces, landings and a small speed-based baseline above about 200 km/h. Shake = trauma² (or ³) times Perlin noise on position (cm-scale) and roll and pitch (≤1-2°). Decay about 1-1.5 trauma per second. Also send matching haptic pulses to the phone.
- **Replay/TV cams**: place trackside camera nodes along the spline (about every 150-300 m and at every corner apex) with a pan-and-zoom-to-follow rule (fixed position, look-at with long focal length, 20-35° FOV), switch to the next node when the car passes, and mix in helicopter, onboard bumper and chase shots. Record replays as car transforms at 30-60 Hz so they cost nothing extra to simulate.
